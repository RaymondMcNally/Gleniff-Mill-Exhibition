02 · What a concept is

One idea that makes the decisions

A building is hundreds of decisions. Without an idea behind them, each is made on its own and the result feels like a collection of parts. A concept is the one idea that the whole project serves. It is a position you take about what matters most in this place, for these people. Once you have it, it makes the later decisions for you: stuck on a window, a step or a roof, you ask what the idea wants.

  1. 1ObserveEverything you found in Stage 1: the water, the slope, the ruins, the light, the sounds, the history.
  2. 2ChoosePick the one thing that matters most and say it in a sentence. That sentence is your concept.
  3. 3Draw itTurn the sentence into a few lines and arrows on the site: a parti.
  4. 4Test itRedraw the parti against different headings. Does it still decide things? Change it until it does.
  5. 5Tell itEvery later decision traces back to the idea, like a storyteller who never loses the thread.

What is a parti?

A parti is the concept drawn. The word comes from the French prendre parti, to take a side or make a decision, and was used at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris for the basic scheme behind a plan. A parti diagram leaves almost everything out. It uses a few lines, a hatch, an arrow, a circle, and shows only how the idea works on the site.

The brief puts it this way: diagrams are usually more concise and precise than words for ideas about architecture, so words support the diagrams, not the other way round. If your diagram needs a paragraph to explain it, simplify the diagram.

What a concept isn't

  • A theme. "Water" or "nature" is a subject. It doesn't decide anything yet.
  • A shape. "A curved roof" is a form. It doesn't say why, or why here.
  • A material or a style. "Timber" or "modern" can serve an idea, but isn't one.
  • A story added afterwards. If the idea is written once the design is done, it isn't driving the design.

03 · Precedents

Three partis, three buildings

Each of these buildings can be drawn in a few lines, and each idea reaches from the whole down to the smallest part. The Sydney Opera House sketches are the ones in the brief; the other two diagrams are ours, drawn to show the idea, not the building. Look the projects up and draw your own.

Sydney Opera House

Jørn Utzon · designed from 1957

The brief's own example of a parti. A breaking wave inspired the minor hall's ceiling and a beech tree's branches the major hall's. The shells float above a heavy stone platform, like clouds over a plateau. The roof's geometry was eventually resolved by cutting every shell from the surface of one sphere. That let the whole roof be built from repeated, prefabricated rib segments and tiles: one idea, from the skyline to the tile.

Brief headings it answers: Form, Rhythm, Structure, Materiality, Approach.

Jean-Marie Tjibaou Cultural Centre

Renzo Piano · New Caledonia · 1998

A line of tall timber "cases" along a path, drawn from the great huts of the Kanak people. The open slatted tops look unfinished, as if still growing. They catch the trade winds and draw air through the rooms. Culture, climate and structure are the same move.

Brief headings it answers: Rhythm, Sequence, Acoustic, Aspect, Structure.

Therme Vals

Peter Zumthor · Switzerland · 1996

The baths are carved from the mountain like a quarry: solid blocks of local stone, with the bathing spaces as the gaps between them. Thin slots of light come down from above. The idea governs the plan, the material, the light and the slow sequence of bathing.

Brief headings it answers: Massing, Materiality, Light, Haptic texture, Sequence.

    Say it in words, on one page

    Your intent is a typed A4 page that states the idea and what it means for the design. The prompts below follow the brief: the identity of the setting, the landscape of learning, the experience of the place, and its form. Use them as a starting point, write in your own voice, and leave out any that don't apply. It saves in this browser as you type.

    08 · Conceptual models

    Think with your hands

    A concept model isn't a small version of the pavilion. It is the idea in material: a fold, a cut, a lift, a sequence of pieces, the play of light on a surface. Make several, fast, then keep the ones that teach you something.

    How to make them

    • Work fast and small. Spend ten minutes on each and make five, not one model that takes a day.
    • One idea per model. If a model tries to show everything, make two.
    • Use honest materials. Card, balsa, wire, clay, plaster, fabric, found material from the site (photographed, not taken). Choose what behaves like your idea: what bends, stacks, carves or lets light through.
    • Test it in light. Put it under a lamp at the sun's angle for the site, and photograph it. Light is one of the things the brief asks your design to draw on, and it is easiest to see this way.
    • Photograph and annotate. The photos go in your sketchbook and on your A2s.

    A piece of the site to build on

    Some concepts need the ground. A small contour base of your part of the site makes the slope, the stream bank or the mill platform real.

    Your model: choose a square and download a base Model kit: the whole site Sun angles for testing in light Heliodon: real shadows on a bench

    For quick concept bases, 1:500 hand-cut grey card or contour layers is plenty. The same 1:500 base can carry your sketch design model at the interim review. Ask your tutor which formats are open this week.

    09 · Story and place

    Weave it like a seanchaí

    The seanchaí was the traditional Irish storyteller, who held a story in memory and told it so the listeners never lost the thread. The brief asks you to weave your concept into the project's narrative in the same way. Gleniff has its own stories: the barytes mine and mill, the water that powered it, the mountains of the Horseshoe valley that enclose it, the townlands around it and their legends. Finding them can give you an idea that belongs to this place and no other.

    What this stage assesses

    From the brief. Stage 2 assesses all eleven; the concept work speaks most to LO2, LO3, LO5 and LO6.

    Learning outcomes

    • LO1 Understand scale and ergonomics and apply them in a design.
    • LO2 Understand the use of form and spatial concepts in a design.
    • LO3 Understand how basic design issues relate to place and identity.
    • LO4 Analyse design typologies and precedents.
    • LO5 Respond to a design brief with evaluation of contextual, environmental, historical and social criteria.
    • LO6 Generate coherent design proposals using fundamental design methodologies.
    • LO7 Show a basic understanding of the integration of materials and structure in a design.
    • LO8 Use a range of communication methods and media to present design proposals clearly.
    • LO9 Use orthographic and paraline drawing conventions at a basic level.
    • LO10 Take part and collaborate actively in teamwork.
    • LO11 Analyse your own learning needs and work independently.
