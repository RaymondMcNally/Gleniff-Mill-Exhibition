ARCT06004 · Project 1 · Stage 2 · Initial and developed design

Design the pavilion.

A temporary pavilion of about 70 m² at Gleniff: a space for learning and recreation, to play, challenge, rest and explore, to dive into fantasy, and to learn the ethical stewardship of the environment. Somewhere one person can be alone and a small group can share what they find, set in a landscape of learning. This page takes you from your concept and sketch diagrams to 1:100 drawings and a 1:50 presentation model, with bases and tools built from the site survey.

    02 · The task

    A space for learning and recreation

    A place to play, challenge, rest and explore, to dive into fantasy, and ultimately to learn about the ethical stewardship of the environment. Design two kinds of space, and the ground around them, in a balanced relationship with the landscape, the physical setting and the broader context.

    An intimate space

    An intimate experience for one person. Somewhere to hide, read, watch the water, listen. Think about how small it can be, what it opens to, and how you know you are alone in it.

    A collective space

    A collective experience for a small group sharing their encounters. A circle, a stage, a table, a hearth? Where does the group gather, and how does the space hold it?

    The approach and the landscape

    An approach and threshold sequence to the pavilion, and a landscape proposal for the ground around it, as part of a landscape of learning.

    What happens there

    The brief lists six things the pavilion should make room for. Your design doesn't need to do all of them equally, but it should know which ones it is for.

    Take your inspiration from this landscape, through materials, textures, natural elements and light, and from what you explored in Stage 1 and in your concept and site strategy. A form, or a series of forms, with a total gross internal area of about 70 m². Gross internal area is the floor area measured inside the external walls, on every level.
    03 · Initial design

    Diagram first, then sketch

    You can't solve function, site, structure and atmosphere at once. Diagrams let you explore one relationship at a time, organisational, experiential and formal, and the sketches grow out of them into an architectural language. Work at 1:500, 1:200 and 1:100, and keep people in every drawing to check the scale and ergonomics.

    MatrixEvery space against every other: which must be next to which?
    BubbleSpaces as bubbles sized by area, arranged by how they relate.
    RelationshipLinks between spaces: strong, weak, visual only, through a threshold.
    ScaleSpaces and figures at the same scale: how big is 70 m² next to a child?

    Relationship diagram

    A starting point for your spatial analysis. The brief asks for relationship diagrams that show context, so the river and the mountains are already on the board: add the mill, the road or the waterfall as more context. Drag the bubbles; click one, then another, to link them, and click a link to make it weaker or remove it. Bubble size follows area. Dashed bubbles are outdoors. Print it, then work over it by hand on trace.

    Interim review: what you bring

    A2 orthographic drawings, hardline in mechanical pencil, plus your iteration process work on A3 clean-cut sketch roll. Freehand mixed media and annotation are encouraged on tracing paper overlays. Tick items off as you go; your ticks stay in this browser.

    05 · Drawing bases

    Bases at the brief's scales

    The downloads above are centred on your pavilion. Print them at 100% (actual size) so the scale is true, then draw over them on trace, or trace the ground onto your heavyweight sheet. Use them as a start: the drawings you hand in are your own, in mechanical pencil.

    1:500 context planA2 landscape, about 285 × 185 m: nearly the whole survey, centred on your pavilion as far as the survey allows. Contours every 1 m, river, stream, road and mill. For the interim context plans. The mountains lie beyond the survey: add them from the OS map and your Stage 1 work.
    1:500 context sectionAlong the same line A–B as the site section. Draw it long, across the valley, for the interim context sections; it drops to 1:1000 if the line is too long for A2.
    1:200 site planA2 landscape, about 110 × 65 m around your pavilion. Contours every 0.5 m, index contours every 2.5 m, river, stream, road and the mill platform. For the interim sketch design plans and the final detailed site plan with roof plan.
    1:200 site sectionAlong the line A–B you draw: the ground from the survey, your pavilion's outline if the line crosses it, a datum and scale figures. For the interim sketch sections, and two for the final review, at right angles to each other.
    1:100 ground planA2 landscape, about 57 × 37 m around your pavilion. Contours every 0.25 m with spot levels, for the detailed 1:100 pavilion plans and for setting floor levels.
    1:50 ground planA2 landscape, about 28 × 16 m. Closer contours and spot levels for building the 1:50 presentation model and its landscape.
    Approach views sheetA2 with six numbered frames and a key plan of your viewpoints, for the sequence of views from the distant view to the threshold.

    The survey is a drone lidar ground model on a 0.4 m grid. At 1:100 and 1:50 the contours are smoothed between survey points: good for design, not a measured survey. Levels are in metres above the lowest ground of the survey.

    06 · Scale and ergonomics

    Design for young and old

    The pavilion is for visitors young and old, on their own and in small groups. Put scale figures in every plan, section and model, and check heights against the people who will use them: a sill a child can see over, a seat their feet reach the ground from, a doorway an adult can still get through.

    WhoHeightEye heightSeat heightTable height

    Heights are rounded averages for Irish and UK children and adults; real children vary a lot at every age. Seat and table heights follow the school furniture sizes in EN 1729-1 for each body height.

    For stairs, ramps, handrails, guarding and level access, use the Building Regulations Technical Guidance Documents K (stairways, ladders, ramps and guards) and M (access and use) when you reach the 1:100 developed design, and talk to your tutor.

    07 · Developed design

    From sketches to a complete proposal

    By now your diagrams and sketches have become scaled proposals. Develop them into one coherent, detailed design: plans, sections and elevations at 1:100, with site plans and site sections at 1:500 and 1:200. Work on context, site, users, spaces and experience as one connected thing, and settle your structure, materials, atmosphere and light.

      08 · Models

      Massing, development, presentation

      Your conceptual model comes first. Working massing models at several scales, and the sketch design model at 1:500 or 1:200 for the interim review, test the form quickly. The final detailed presentation model at 1:50 sits on its contextual landscape, labelled, with a north point.

      A 1:50 landscape base

      A contour model of the ground under and around your pavilion at 1:50, cut on the laser from board. Centred on your footprint; set the size and board here. The ZIP holds SVG and DXF sheets for the laser and a list of layers to stack.

      Place your pavilion first (section 04); the base is centred on it, or on the mill if you haven't.

      Making and photographing

      • Give every model a solid base, and label the underside with your name, the stage and the scale.
      • Put people in it. Use the 1:50 cut-out figures from section 06.
      • Photograph on white or black. A neutral background, nothing else in the frame.
      • Light it on purpose. One lamp at the sun's angle for Gleniff shows form best. Try low winter sun and high summer sun; the Sun page gives the angles.
      • Try angles and framing. An eye-level view from a child's or an adult's height, an aerial view, a close-up of the threshold. Colour or black and white?
      09 · Precedents

      Places for play and learning, and how they were drawn

      Look these up, then ask the same question of each: what did the designer decide, and how does the building or landscape let its visitors discover it? The diagrams are ours, to show the idea; the O'Donnell + Tuomey drawing is from the brief.

      Fuji Kindergarten

      Tezuka Architects · Tokyo · 2007

      An oval ring of classrooms with a flat roof the children can run around without end, with trees growing up through the deck. The building itself is the playground.

      Ask: can your roof, wall or ground be played on?

      Amsterdam playgrounds

      Aldo van Eyck · Amsterdam · 1947–1978

      Hundreds of small playgrounds in leftover corners of the city, made from a few simple elements: sandpits, stepping stones, climbing frames. Children decide how to use them.

      Ask: what few simple things could make a place for play?

      Hortus Conclusus

      Peter Zumthor · Serpentine Pavilion, London · 2011

      A black timber box entered through dark corridors, opening on to a garden open to the sky. The pavilion is a threshold: it takes you out of the park so you can find a garden inside it.

      Ask: how does your approach and threshold change what is found inside?

      Baltic Street Adventure Playground

      Assemble · Glasgow · 2013–

      An adventure playground run with local children, who build, change and look after it themselves with loose materials, tools and fire. Making the place is the activity.

      Ask: what could children make, change or look after at Gleniff?

      Ireland at the Venice Biennale

      O'Donnell + Tuomey · 2004

      Ireland's exhibit at the 2004 Biennale presented their transformation of the former industrial school at Letterfrack, Co. Galway, into a furniture college. The brief opens with final design drawings captioned as theirs (top of this page): look at how plan, perspective and model tell one story.

      Ask: how will your drawings work together on the wall?

      10 · Portfolio

      Present the whole semester

      Revise the design after the interim and final reviews, then present the finished scheme, drawings and models, and all your process work, sketchbooks and sketch models, clearly in your portfolio case.

      What the portfolio should show

      Laying out a sheet

      • Structure and layout. Decide the grid before you draw. Line up drawings that relate: plan above section, elevations side by side.
      • Visual hierarchy. One drawing leads each sheet. The eye should know where to start.
      • Information. Every drawing has a title, scale bar and north point, and a legend where needed. Sheet titles carry all the key facts.
      • Lettering. Consistent architectural lettering or one clear typeface, at a few sizes.
      • Negative space. Leave room. A crowded sheet is hard to read.
      • Legibility. Line weights that separate cut from beyond; render that helps, not hides.

      Competition boards by well-known architects are good references for layout.

      11 · Learning outcomes and reading

      What this stage assesses

      From the brief.

      • LO1 Understand scale and ergonomics and apply them in a design.
      • LO2 Understand the use of form and spatial concepts in a design.
      • LO3 Understand how basic design issues relate to place and identity.
      • LO4 Analyse design typologies and precedents.
      • LO5 Respond to a design brief with evaluation of contextual, environmental, historical and social criteria.
      • LO6 Generate coherent design proposals using fundamental design methodologies.
      • LO7 Show a basic understanding of the integration of materials and structure in a design.
      • LO8 Use a range of communication methods and media to present design proposals clearly.
      • LO9 Use orthographic and paraline drawing conventions at a basic level.
      • LO10 Take part and collaborate actively in teamwork.
      • LO11 Analyse your own learning needs and work independently.
